# Writing Well

Take a piece of writing the user already has and make it clearer, tighter, and stronger, using the principles William Zinsser laid out in *On Writing Well*. The aim is not to make the text sound like Zinsser, or like Claude, or like every other edited text in the world. The aim is to remove everything that gets between the writer and the reader, so the writer's own thinking and voice come through with more force.

## The one idea underneath everything

Simplicity. Clutter is the enemy — the redundant word, the pompous phrase, the qualifier that dilutes, the passive that hides who did what. Most first drafts can lose a good fraction of their words with no loss of meaning, and the meaning usually gets *clearer* when they go. So the core move is subtraction: for every word, ask whether it earns its place. If a shorter word will do, use it. If a word can go without changing the meaning, cut it.

But subtraction has a limit, and it matters: **do not sand the person out of their own prose.** Warmth, personality, rhythm, humor, a deliberate long sentence that builds — these are not clutter. A cold, correct, characterless paragraph is a failure even if every word is "necessary." Clean the writing; keep the human.

## Before you touch a word

1. **Read the whole thing first.** Understand what the piece is trying to do, who it's for, and what the writer's natural voice sounds like. You can't tighten prose well until you know what it's supposed to carry.
2. **Gauge how heavy an edit is wanted.** If the user said ("just tighten it," "light touch," "gut it," "make it half as long"), follow that. If they didn't say, default to a *firm but faithful* edit: cut the clutter and fix the weak constructions, but keep the structure, the content, and the voice. When in doubt on a big piece, do a firm edit on the first paragraph or two, show it, and ask if that's the right level before doing the rest.
3. **Never change the writer's meaning or invent facts.** Editing tightens *how* something is said; it never quietly alters *what* is said. If a sentence is unclear because you can't tell what the writer meant, don't guess — flag it and ask, or offer the most likely reading as a question.

## The editing passes

Work through these. They overlap, and you don't need rigid separate passes, but this is the order of priority.

### 1. Cut the clutter
This is the biggest win. Remove words and phrases that add length but no meaning:
- **Padding and throat-clearing:** "it should be pointed out that," "the fact that," "in a very real sense," "for all intents and purposes," "needless to say," "I might add."
- **Bloated phrases that have a one-word equal:** "at this point in time" → *now*; "in the event that" → *if*; "in order to" → *to*; "due to the fact that" → *because*; "a large number of" → *many*; "has the ability to" → *can*.
- **Redundancies** where one word already contains the other: "personal friend," "free gift," "end result," "future plans," "collaborate together," "past history," "completely eliminate."
See `references/clutter-and-fixes.md` for a fuller lookup list — consult it whenever you want to be systematic.

### 2. Make the verbs do the work — and go active
- **Prefer active voice.** "The committee approved the plan" beats "The plan was approved by the committee." Active is shorter, clearer, and tells the reader who acted. Passive is fine when the actor is genuinely unknown or unimportant, but most passive is just fog.
- **Use strong, precise verbs** instead of a weak verb propped up by other words. "She examined the data" beats "She conducted an examination of the data." "He decided" beats "He made a decision."
- **Kill "there is / there are / it is" openers** when they bury the real subject. "There are three reasons this fails" → "This fails for three reasons." "It is important that you sign" → "You must sign."

### 3. Cut the qualifiers and hedges
Words like *very, rather, quite, really, somewhat, a bit, a little, sort of, kind of, pretty (much), in a sense, basically, actually, essentially* almost always weaken a sentence. They're the writer flinching. "This is a very interesting result" is weaker than "This is an interesting result" — and if it's only interesting because you said "very," it isn't. Prune them hard. The same goes for over-hedging ("it could perhaps be argued that this might possibly suggest") — say what you mean.

### 4. Trim adverbs and adjectives that aren't pulling weight
- **Cut adverbs that just repeat the verb.** "Smiled happily," "shouted loudly," "gripped tightly," "clenched tightly" — the verb already carries it.
- **Cut adjectives that state a quality the noun already implies.** "Yellow daffodils," "tall skyscraper," "the brown dirt" — unless the color or size is actually doing work in the sentence.
- Keep the adjective or adverb when it adds real information or a needed image. The test is whether the sentence loses something true when you remove it.

### 5. Plain words over fancy ones
Zinsser's rule: never use a long word where a short one works, and never reach for jargon to sound important. "Use," not "utilize." "Help," not "facilitate." "Enough," not "a sufficient amount of." "Buy," not "purchase." "Show," not "demonstrate." Business, academic, and corporate writing especially fills up with concept-nouns and jargon ("leverage synergies," "operationalize," "impactful"). Translate it back into words a person would actually say. See the reference file for a substitution list.

### 6. One thought at a time
Long sentences aren't wrong, but a sentence that tries to hold three ideas at once usually loses the reader. When a sentence sprawls, break it. Read for the point where the reader would have to stop and reassemble the meaning — and put a period there. Short sentences also give you rhythm and let you land a point hard. (Vary length, though; all-short is choppy.)

### 7. Check the opening and the ending
- **The first sentence carries the most weight.** It has one job: make the reader want the second sentence. Cut any warm-up that delays the real start — writers often bury their true opening in the second or third paragraph. If so, promote it.
- **End with intent.** Good endings are often short and slightly surprising, and they stop *while the reader still wants more*, rather than trailing off into summary and "in conclusion." When the piece has said what it came to say, stop.

### 8. Unity and consistency
Keep the piece consistent in tense, in person (don't drift between "you" and "one" and "we"), and in tone (don't lurch from formal to breezy without reason). A reader feels these seams even when they can't name them.

### 9. Read it for the ear
Prose has sound and rhythm even on the page. After editing, read the result through as if aloud. If you stumble, the reader will too — fix the spot. Watch for unintended rhymes, tongue-twisters, and clusters of the same sound. Contractions ("it's," "don't," "you're") usually make prose sound more human, not less correct — keep them unless the register is formal.

## What not to do

- **Don't homogenize.** Never rewrite a distinctive voice into generic clean prose. If the writer is funny, keep them funny. If they favor a certain cadence, respect it.
- **Don't over-cut into coldness.** Not every extra word is clutter; some are breath, some are warmth. Tightening should make the piece feel *more* alive, not embalmed.
- **Don't "correct" deliberate choices** — dialect, a sentence fragment used for effect, a repeated word used as a drumbeat, an unusual but intentional structure.
- **Don't add clutter of your own.** No throat-clearing preamble, no padding the explanation.
- **Don't change facts, claims, names, or the writer's argument.** When you're unsure what they meant, ask instead of smoothing over it.

## How to present the result

Default output:

1. **The revised text** — clean, ready to use.
2. **What I changed** — a short section, grouped by the kind of change, with two or three concrete *before → after* examples so the writer sees the pattern and can do it themselves next time. Don't list every micro-edit; show the recurring moves ("I cut the qualifiers throughout — e.g., *very unique* → *unique*"). Explaining the *why* briefly (Zinsser's reasoning) helps the writer more than the fix alone.
3. **Offer next steps** — e.g., "Want a heavier cut, a lighter touch, or a marked-up version showing exactly what came out?"

Adapt to the request: if the user only wants the clean version with no commentary, give just that. If they want to learn, lean into the explanations.

### Optional: the bracket (marked-up) mode
If the user wants to *see* the clutter come out — which is a great way to learn — reproduce their original with the words you'd cut wrapped in [square brackets], the way Zinsser marked up his own drafts, then give the clean version below it. This makes the surgery visible.

**Example of bracket mode:**

Original with cuts marked:
> [The fact that] [basically] the report [in question] was [very] late [in terms of its timing] caused [a certain amount of] problems [for the team].

Clean version:
> The late report caused problems for the team.

## Scope note
These principles were written for nonfiction — essays, articles, business and expository writing, memoir, journalism — and that's where they apply most directly. They still help almost any prose, but use judgment with fiction, poetry, or highly stylized writing, where a "qualifier" or a long sentence may be doing deliberate artistic work. When in doubt, err toward preserving the writer's intent.
